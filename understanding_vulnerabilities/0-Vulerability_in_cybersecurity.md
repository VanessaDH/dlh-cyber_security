What is a vulnerability?
A weak spot in a system that someone could use to break in, steal data, or cause damage.

Why is it more than just a technical weakness?
Because a flaw alone isn't dangerous — it only becomes a real problem once someone figures out how to use it against you. It's a mix of a mistake and bad intent.

How do vulnerabilities lead to breaches?
Attackers find a weak spot and use it to get in. Find the flaw + exploit it = breach. That's basically how most hacks happen.

Historical context, in short:
Early on, bugs were just seen as annoying glitches, not real threats — computers weren't connected to much. Once the internet connected everything (the 1988 Morris Worm was an early wake-up call), attacks got smarter and more common, so vulnerabilities became a serious, ongoing concern instead of a minor one.

Main types:

Software – bugs in code
Hardware – flaws in chips (e.g., Spectre/Meltdown)
Network – bad setups, like open ports or weak firewalls
Human – phishing, careless clicks, insider mistakes

Why it matters for tech companies:
An unfixed vulnerability can mean stolen data, lost money, and damaged trust. Companies manage this by patching (updating software fast), doing regular audits (checking for weak spots), and penetration testing (paying people to try to break in on purpose).
